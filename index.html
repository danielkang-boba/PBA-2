Passion or Addiction?

I found love on January 1st, 2026, and it was waiting for me in my study.

The morning was still gray when I opened the door. On the desk sat a computer built around an NVIDIA GeForce 3070, eight gigabytes of memory, and a monitor so sleek it seemed to hum with its own private light. "Wha—" was all I managed.

"Do you like it?" My brother stood in the doorway, grinning like a magician who had just finished his best trick.

I had no answer. It was the first time in my life I had been speechless. I stood frozen, afraid that if I blinked it would vanish, that someone had made a mistake and this gift belonged to a luckier person. "It's beautiful," I finally whispered.

After that, my days rearranged themselves around it. I woke and went to it before I had fully stopped dreaming, and I played for hours until the window went dark. At night, it followed me into sleep. In that single week, a quiet obsession took root.

But the obsession was never really about the computer. It was about what the computer meant: the innovation, the adaptability, the limitless doors that opened every time I pressed a key. It felt like a tool carried here from another world, and I was drawn to it like a moth to a flame.

And like the moth, I didn't notice the heat at first. Curiosity gave me a hundred small gifts. I learned how the parts inside the machine talked to one another. I learned why a game stuttered and how to fix it. Every answer felt like a key, and every key opened a door with three more behind it. But the same hunger that fed me was also consuming me. Dinners went cold. Messages from friends sat unanswered. Homework waited while I chased one more question down one more rabbit hole. Somewhere along the way, I had stopped exploring the world and started disappearing from it.

Months later, I began to wonder what exactly I had caught. Was this passion, or was it an addiction? And underneath that question sat a stranger one. Was I drawn to computers because I was already the kind of person who loses hours to the way things work? Or had the computers made me that person?

The moment I finally saw myself clearly was small. My brother, the same one who had stood grinning in the doorway, asked me how the computer actually worked. I opened my mouth to answer and found that I had a mountain of knowledge and no way to hand any of it to him. I fumbled through jargon while his eyes glazed over. All that curiosity, and I had kept it locked in my own head, like a library with no door.

That was when I understood the difference between collecting knowledge and using it. So I tried something harder than learning: I tried explaining. I slowed down. I used comparisons, like calling memory a desk and storage a filing cabinet. Watching understanding spread across my brother's face felt better than any answer I had found alone. My curiosity hadn't shrunk. It had grown a purpose.

It also changed how I thought about fitting in. For years I had treated my intensity as a flaw, something to hide so I could blend in with people who seemed content to skim the surface of things. Conformity offered a quiet bargain: dim the thing that makes you different, and the world will make room for you. But I had it backward. The problem was never the depth of my curiosity, it was keeping it to myself. Being an individual, I realized, does not require a grand act of rebellion. It only requires following your attention honestly, and then having the courage to bring others along.

I still can't say whether I love computers because of who I am, or whether I am who I am because I love them. I've stopped needing to know. What I learned that morning, standing speechless in the gray light, is that a consuming curiosity is both a flame and a lamp. Left alone, it burns. Shared, it lights the way. It is the most honest part of me, and I'm finally learning to carry it well.
